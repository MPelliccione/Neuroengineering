## Part II, Problem A (Astolfi): disorders of consciousness

**A1. Mistake (2 pts).**
The networks are built with **Ordinary Coherence**, which is symmetric (Cxy = Cyx) and therefore **undirected**: it only tells whether two signals are linearly correlated at a frequency, not who drives whom. Both indices chosen to separate MCS from UWS are **directional**: the "sum of connections *directed* from frontal to parietal regions" and the frontal **out-degree** (number of edges *leaving* a node). On an undirected (symmetric) adjacency matrix an edge F–P is the same as P–F and out-degree = in-degree = degree, so neither index can be computed meaningfully: the "fronto-parietal influence" would just be fronto-parietal coupling. In addition, coherence is **bivariate**, so with 64 channels it is exposed to spurious links from common/hidden sources and volume conduction.
*Sources:* A-L7 p13, p18, p21 (OC symmetric, no direction, bivariate); A-L10 p17–18 (out-degree is defined for directed graphs); same reasoning as Key Sep20 A3. ✅

**A2. Improvement (2 pts).**
Use a **directed, multivariate, spectral** estimator: **Partial Directed Coherence (PDC)** (or DTF) computed on an **MVAR** model fitted to all channels.
- *Directed* (PDCᵢⱼ ≠ PDCⱼᵢ): the fronto→parietal flow and the out-degree become well defined.
- *Spectral*: it keeps the analysis in the four bands (delta, theta, alpha, beta) the study already uses, which the Granger test (time domain) would lose.
- *Multivariate*: it accounts for all recorded channels together, reducing spurious links from hidden/common sources compared to bivariate methods.
- Limitation to manage: an MVAR on 64 channels needs a lot of data. Resting-state recordings can be made long enough (patients do not have to perform a task), or the channels can be grouped into regions of interest to reduce the model size. Statistical validation of the links (e.g. against a null distribution) should precede graph indices.

*Sources:* A-L9 p3, p13–16 (PDC, MVAR in frequency domain); A-L8 p14 (Granger is time domain); Key Jul20 A1 and Key Jan21 A1 (PDC needs long data; GT robust to short data). 🟡 Granger test is an acceptable alternative per Key Sep20 A3, but loses the frequency bands; PDC is the better fit here.

**A3. Which index (1.5 pts).**
Choose the **fronto-parietal influence** (AUC = 0.8). The AUC measures how well an index discriminates the two classes over all thresholds: 0.5 = chance (diagonal), 1 = perfect. 0.8 is good discrimination, while 0.6 is close to chance, so the frontal out-degree would misclassify many patients.
*Sources:* A-L6 p12 (ROC, AUC), self-test p18; Key Sep20 A15–A16 (best curve is the one far from the diagonal; AUC ∝ discriminability). ✅

## Part II, Problem B (Cincotti): motor imagery EEG

**B1. Raw vs filtered (2 pts).**
```
S1 = raw
S2 = alpha (mu, 8–13 Hz)
S3 = beta (14–30 Hz)
```
S1 is the only trace with slow drifts and a broadband, irregular shape (in trial A, Cz and C3 drift by tens of µV over seconds, likely eye/movement artifact), i.e. it contains all frequencies: it is the unfiltered signal. S2 and S3 are zero-mean narrow-band oscillations, as produced by a band-pass filter. Counting cycles, S2 has ~10–11 oscillations per 1000 ms (≈10 Hz), the alpha band; since the electrodes are central (Cz, C3), this is the **mu** rhythm. S3 oscillates about twice as fast (≈20–25 Hz, ~5 cycles in a 200 ms burst) and appears in short bursts: **beta** band. Neither looks like delta/theta (would be 1–7 cycles per second) or gamma (>30 Hz, much denser).
*Sources:* C-1.1 p7–8 (rhythm bands; mu at central sites, arc-shaped, ~10 Hz); Key Jun20 B2 (recognising mu/alpha/EMG traces by eye). ⚠️ Read from the figure; the bands follow from my cycle count on the 300 dpi render.

**B2. Which task per trial (2 pts).**
```
Trial A = R
Trial B = F
```
Motor imagery causes an ERD (power decrease) of mu and beta over the cortex that represents the imagined body part, following the somatotopic (Penfield homunculus) map. The right hand is represented in the **left** sensorimotor cortex, under **C3**; the feet are represented near the midline (mesial cortex) under **Cz**.
- Trial A: mu (S2) and beta (S3) are clearly attenuated at **C3** while still large at Cz ⇒ ERD over the left hand area ⇒ **right hand**.
- Trial B: the opposite. Mu/beta are small at **Cz** and large at C3 (the hand area even synchronises, a typical "focal ERD / surround ERS" pattern during foot imagery) ⇒ **feet**.

*Sources:* C-1.3 p16–17 (ERD = power reduction during movement); A-L3 p15 (somatotopy, contralateral control); Key Ex B4 ("mu desynchronizes when the right hand movement is imagined, so it must be recorded on the left hemisphere", i.e. C3); C-2.5 p12, p21 (MI-based BCI, ERD-ERS). ⚠️ Depends on reading amplitudes in the figure; the C3/Cz contrast is clear, but the "surround ERS" part is from general knowledge, not the slides.

**B3. Which spectrum (1.5 pts).**
```
Spectrum A = DEF
Spectrum B = APM
```
The periodogram computed from the definition |DFT(x)|² on the whole recording has a very fine frequency resolution (Δf = 1/T, T long) but a very **high variance** at each frequency bin: the estimate does not improve with a longer signal, so it looks like dense, spiky "grass" (Spectrum A). Welch's averaged periodogram splits the signal into shorter (windowed, possibly overlapping) segments, computes a periodogram on each and averages them: the variance drops by roughly the number of segments, at the cost of a coarser resolution (Δf = 1/segment length). Hence the smooth, lower-resolution curve with clearly visible broad peaks (delta, ~7 Hz/alpha and ~20 Hz/beta bumps) is Spectrum B.
*Sources:* C-2.4 p8–9 ("the averaged periodogram method trades off spectral resolution with lower estimate variability"); C-2.3 p13, p22; C-TS p11; Key Jul20 B20. ✅
